Model Conceptual Foundations & Core Finding

The Hattie & Donoghue (2016) Learning Model demonstrates that learning strategies are not universally effective in isolation, but are highly dependent on the student’s specific phase in the learning cycle. Synthesizing over 228 meta-analyses spanning 18,956+ studies, the model organizes learning into three primary phases: Surface, Deep, and Transfer, each split into an acquisition and consolidation phase. Crucially, the authors prove that high-level thinking strategies (like problem-based learning or peer tutoring) often backfire when introduced prior to acquiring sufficient surface knowledge, whereas low-level strategies (like highlighting or mnemonics) excel during initial exposure but diminish as deep understanding is forged.

The Core Problem in Study Skills Training

For decades, educators and study-skills programs have attempted to teach generic "learning strategies" or "critical thinking skills" as standalone courses. Hattie & Donoghue’s synthesis proves that generic strategy training taught out of context yields disappointing results. Metacognition and learning strategies are not content-free; critical thinking in mathematics (e.g., checking that step-by-step logic avoids division by zero) is fundamentally different from critical thinking in history (e.g., assessing source bias and historical context).

"Knowing When to Hold 'Em, Knowing When to Fold 'Em"

The central thesis of the model is that strategy effectiveness depends entirely on when during the learning cycle a strategy is invoked:

  • Surface Acquiring Phase: Strategies like outlining, summarization, and note-taking assist learners in acquiring vocabulary and basic facts. Overlearning surface facts frees working memory space for complex reasoning.
  • Surface Consolidating Phase: Spaced practice, deliberate practice, and receiving feedback build retrieval strength so knowledge becomes easily accessible.
  • Deep Acquiring Phase: Metacognitive planning, concept mapping, and self-regulation allow students to seek relational meaning and connect new concepts to prior schema.
  • Deep Consolidating Phase: Dialogue, peer tutoring, classroom discussion, and self-explanation enable students to test their conceptual structures and "speak the language of learning."
  • Transfer Phase: Explicitly teaching students to detect similarities and differences between known problems and novel tasks leads to dramatic transfer gains (d = 1.32).

Why Advanced Strategies (PBL, Inquiry) Fail Prematurely

The model resolves the paradox of why methods like Problem-Based Learning (PBL, d = 0.15) or Inquiry-Based Teaching (d = 0.31) produce surprisingly small overall effect sizes. When novice learners are placed in unstructured problem-based environments without prior surface knowledge, they engage in backward reasoning (working from unknown to known), leading to high error rates and cognitive overload. When PBL is applied after students possess solid surface knowledge, forward reasoning occurs, and outcomes skyrocket.
